A scenic tour and a flying lesson are the same forty minutes.

If you came to the Keys on vacation, take the tour. If the tour got in your head, that is what the discovery flight is for. Nobody here will push you from one to the other.

Every tour we fly follows a route a student flies too. Sombrero Reef, the Seven Mile Bridge, Bahia Honda, Duck Key. The airplane is the same. The water underneath is the same. The view out the window is the same.

The difference is who is holding the controls, and whether it goes in a logbook. That is why a lot of people who came here for a $299 sightseeing flight came back for a discovery flight, and a few of them are pilots now.

Both start the same way: you call, you say roughly when you are around, and somebody who actually flies these airplanes tells you what is possible that week.

N67496 · Consolidated Vultee BT-13A · 1942

The Vultee Vibrator.

Not a fighter. A classroom.

Almost every American pilot who flew in the Second World War went through a BT-13. It sat in the middle of the training pipeline, after the Stearman and before the AT-6: the first airplane with real horsepower, a constant-speed prop and a radio, and It shook enough at certain power settings to earn its nickname.

Which makes it the right airplane for this place. Eighty years on, the BT-13 is still doing the job it was built for, at a flight school, teaching people to fly. Ours is a restored 1942 airframe with a Pratt & Whitney R-985 radial and its training-command markings still on the tail.

"I believe there's a fountain of youth somewhere in here."

Sol Bradman, owner, on why he keeps teaching. Quoted in Keys Weekly.

Sol Bradman has been flying in the Florida Keys since 1984. He instructed at this academy before he bought it, and has owned and run it since November 2020. The office is upstairs at the EAA Air Museum at Marathon airport, which tells you most of what you need to know about how this place operates.

It is a small operation on purpose. You are taught by the person who owns the airplanes, not assigned to whichever instructor is rotating through on the way to an airline seniority list. There are no deposits and no enrolment fees. You pay as you progress, and if life gets in the way for three months, your money is not sitting in someone else's account.

The other half of what happens here has nothing to do with revenue. The academy runs Young Eagles flights for kids aged 8 to 17, has helped set up an aviation club at Key West High School, and has put Keys teenagers into the left seat who have since gone on to instrument ratings and flight instructor certificates before they finished school.

What that looks like in practice

  • 200+ kids flown free each year through Young Eagles, twice yearly, ages 8 to 17.
  • Aviation club at Key West High School, set up with the academy's help.
  • No deposits. Pay as you progress, not up front.
  • Buzza Foundation scholarships have funded local students training here.
  • Marathon is untowered. You will learn to fit into real traffic on your own radio calls.

It is a fair question and the answer is no. Age of airframe is not what makes a training aircraft safe. Every aircraft on the line is maintained under FAA rules and inspected on the same schedule as any other aircraft in service, and the Cessna 172 is one of the most proven training designs ever built. What an older fleet does change is what you learn: steam gauges, stick and rudder, and a paper chart, which makes you a better pilot than an autopilot and a moving map do.
